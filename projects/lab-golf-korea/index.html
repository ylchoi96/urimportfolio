Product Design Case Study · L.A.B. GOLF KOREA

Bringing L.A.B. GOLF
to the Korean market.

L.A.B. GOLF was already established in the U.S. when an official distribution agreement opened the Korean market. The launch needed more than a translated storefront: Korean customers needed a clear way to understand the brand, compare specialized products, configure custom putters, and move from online discovery to offline fitting.

01 · Context

A new market required a new decision-making experience.

The Korean site could not simply mirror the U.S. store. Product education, purchasing conventions, content hierarchy, custom options, and fitting services needed to work as one localized commerce experience.

I worked across the structure, interface, and implementation — connecting product discovery, configuration, service content, and reservation touchpoints inside a live Cafe24 environment.

02 · Evidence & Constraints

Grounding decisions in the realities of the launch.

This was a production launch rather than a greenfield research project. I used the available business, product, service, and platform evidence to define the design constraints.

Product evidence

High-consideration product structure

Multiple putter models, shafts, dimensions, specifications, and custom choices created a dense decision space.

Service evidence

Fitting is part of the purchase journey

Customers may need expert fitting before purchase, so store information and booking could not remain disconnected from commerce.

Operational evidence

Live Cafe24 constraints

The interface had to work with real product, option, content, and maintenance constraints rather than an unconstrained prototype.

Research note: I did not conduct formal generative user interviews for this launch. I am separating observed project constraints from assumptions rather than presenting unperformed research as evidence. A future validation plan is outlined later in this case study.

04 · Experience Strategy

Design the decision journey, not just the screens.

I structured the experience around the sequence of questions a customer needs to answer: What is L.A.B. GOLF? Which model fits my needs? What do the options mean? Do I need fitting? How do I purchase or book a visit?

05.1 · Product Discovery

Desktop for comparison.
Mobile for focus.

I did not treat mobile as a scaled-down desktop page. Desktop preserves comparison density, while mobile prioritizes category navigation, product recognition, and the next useful action.

Design decision

Keep the product model visible and comparable on desktop; reduce peripheral controls and emphasize scanning on mobile.

05.2 · Custom Configuration

Reduce choice overload with progressive steps.

Custom putters require many interdependent decisions. I separated the configuration into Foundation → Function → Form → Review so users can make one category of decision at a time.

UX rationale

Keep progress, selected values, and price feedback visible so the user can understand both current state and purchase consequences.

Trade-off considered: a single-page configuration can reduce the number of clicks, but exposes many decisions at once. The staged pattern adds steps in exchange for clearer state visibility and lower cognitive load. This rationale should be validated with task-completion testing.

05.3 · Fitting Journey

Build confidence before asking users to book.

Fitting is a service decision, not just a CTA. The experience explains the service and physical touchpoints before sending users into the booking platform.

Conversion touchpoint

From product understanding to an actual fitting reservation.

After learning about the service and location, users move into Naver Booking for the actual reservation. The goal is to make the outbound transition feel like the next step in the same journey.

05.4 · AI-assisted Production

Fill production gaps without losing the brand language.

Some colour-edition and campaign sections did not have enough supplied production imagery. I used AI-assisted image creation as part of the art-direction workflow, then selected, refined, and adapted the outputs for live promotional pages.

AI supported production; it did not replace design decisions. I defined the visual brief first — purpose, composition, colour language, and placement — then generated options, selected viable outputs, refined them, and applied them to the interface.

06 · Accessibility & QA

Accessibility is an implementation requirement, not a visual afterthought.

The project was not a formal WCAG audit, so I do not present it as one. These were the accessibility and quality considerations used as an implementation baseline.

Readable hierarchy

Maintain legible text sizes, predictable hierarchy, and sufficient spacing across desktop and mobile.

Contrast & states

Keep key text and action states distinguishable against high-contrast black/white brand surfaces.

Touch & focus

Use practical touch target sizing on mobile and preserve visible interactive/focus states where the platform allows.

Semantic content

Use meaningful headings, labels, alt text, and semantic HTML where possible within the Cafe24 theme structure.

Responsive QA

Review layouts across breakpoints for clipping, option visibility, content reflow, and purchase-state regressions.

A future accessibility pass should include a documented WCAG 2.2 AA audit with keyboard and assistive-technology testing.

07 · Measurement & Validation

Be explicit about what is known — and what still needs validation.

Reliable pre/post analytics were not available for attribution, so this case study does not claim fabricated conversion uplift. Instead, I define the measures and tests I would use to evaluate the decisions.

Behavioural metrics to track

  • Product listing → product detail click-through
  • Custom configuration start and completion rate
  • Drop-off by configuration step
  • Fitting CTA and outbound booking click-through
  • Option-related customer-service questions

Validation I would run next

  • 5–8 first-time customer usability sessions
  • Model-comparison comprehension tasks
  • Custom-option task completion and error review
  • Fitting-service comprehension and booking intent
  • Keyboard / screen-reader accessibility review

Why include this? A production designer should be able to separate shipped output from validated impact. This measurement plan makes the next learning loop explicit without inventing evidence that the project did not collect.
